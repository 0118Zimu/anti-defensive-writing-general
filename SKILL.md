# Anti-Defensive Writing

Use this skill when the user asks to draft, revise, or review prose that is weakened by unnecessary defensive framing. The goal is direct, well-calibrated writing, not greater certainty or force for its own sake. Follow the requested language, audience, format, and level of intervention.

These safeguards are conditional. Do not impose research-specific audits, evidence labels, status categories, or formal caveats on ordinary prose when they do not serve the task.

## Working principles

- Lead with the answer, finding, position, or action when the genre calls for it. Remove ritual apologies, pre-emptive rebuttals, repeated disclaimers, and stacked hedges that add no useful meaning.
- Define scope positively and precisely where useful. Keep a necessary boundary attached to the claim it qualifies; do not hide or dilute a material limitation.
- Calibrate confidence to the available basis. Retain uncertainty that reflects evidence, method, scope, law, safety, or genuine unknowns; remove only redundant uncertainty language.
- Preserve the source's meaning and commitments. Do not add facts, citations, results, promises, causal claims, or broader applicability to make prose sound stronger.
- Match the document's structure and audience. Do not force every limitation into a dedicated section or add a formal audit when the requested output is a short, ordinary text.

## Concise workflow

1. Identify the requested deliverable, audience, genre, language, and editing depth. Preserve the user's requested structure unless a change would clearly improve the result.
2. Separate empty defensive language from meaningful qualifications, factual claims, constraints, and commitments. Keep the latter; remove or consolidate only the former.
3. When the task contains evidence-based claims or asks for verification, check material claims against the supplied sources or records. Mark claims that are unsupported, ambiguous, overstated, or not verifiable from the supplied material. Do not imply external verification that was not performed.
4. Rewrite for directness and flow. State the supported point first, define the relevant scope, and place necessary qualifications where readers need them.
5. Compare the revision with the source for changes to facts, numbers, dates, units, references, obligations, certainty, and status. Correct unintended changes and flag unresolved issues briefly.

## Conditional evidence safeguards

Apply only the safeguards relevant to the text:

- **Claims and evidence:** For research, evaluation, policy, technical, or other evidence-bearing writing, connect each material claim to the evidence provided. Distinguish evidence from interpretation, recommendation, and assumption where that distinction affects the conclusion. Do not fabricate support or turn correlation, association, or a model result into a stronger causal claim.
- **Unfavorable results and trade-offs:** When comparative metrics or outcomes are reported, preserve relevant gains and declines with their comparator and context. Avoid selective reporting and blanket superiority claims unless the evidence supports them. Describe a supported trade-off neutrally, without apologizing for it or disguising it.
- **Work status:** When describing work, milestones, or plans, distinguish completed, ongoing, and planned actions as applicable. Never present a proposed or in-progress result as completed, or imply a future outcome is already established.
- **Evidence type:** When the distinction matters, identify whether support comes from simulation or model output, measurement or observation, offline evaluation, or use in a live setting. Do not imply real-world measurement or deployment from simulated or offline evidence. Use only distinctions supported by the supplied context.
- **Material limits:** Preserve limitations that affect validity, reproducibility, safety, privacy, legal or policy compliance, or a reader's decision. State the concrete boundary and consequence when known; do not add generic caveats merely to sound cautious.

## Adapt by writing context

Use the same principles with genre-appropriate emphasis:

- **Academic writing:** Keep claims, methods, findings, uncertainty, and limitations aligned with the evidence and study design.
- **Professional writing:** Make decisions, recommendations, progress, and requests easy to find; preserve commitments and dependencies.
- **Technical writing:** Retain prerequisites, conditions, prohibitions, parameters, and failure cases that change how a procedure or specification works.
- **Policy writing:** Separate observed evidence, estimates, value judgments, recommendations, and implementation constraints when relevant.
- **Grant and proposal writing:** State aims and rationale confidently while keeping feasibility, preliminary support, dependencies, and proposed work accurate.
- **Presentations:** Make the main point easy to scan; retain enough context for figures, comparisons, uncertainty, and status to remain interpretable.

## Output modes

Choose the lightest mode that satisfies the request:

1. **Revised text:** Return the polished text directly when the user asks for a rewrite or the task is straightforward.
2. **Revision with notes:** Add brief notes only for material changes, unresolved evidence gaps, or assumptions that affect meaning.
3. **Claim audit:** When requested or useful for an evidence-heavy review, provide a compact table with claim, available support, issue, and suggested treatment.
4. **Alternatives:** Offer distinct versions only when the user requests them or tone choices materially affect the result.

Do not narrate routine editing decisions. Ask for missing source material only when it is necessary to make a factual judgment; otherwise revise what is supported and identify the limit succinctly.
